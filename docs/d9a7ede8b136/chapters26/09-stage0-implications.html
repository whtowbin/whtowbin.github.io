10  Stage 0 and implications

Stage 0 is the lithospheric source: the water the xenolith minerals held in the mantle before the host magma picked them up. This chapter asks what of it survives in the Sullivan Ranch (SR) xenoliths, what that means for xenolith water studies elsewhere, and what the xenoliths are still good for.

10.1 What survives of the mantle water?

Nothing measurable in these xenoliths. The chain of evidence has three links, each from the preceding chapters.

  1. Time was ample. The crystals sat in the carrier melt for at least 3.6 h (shortest Fe–Mg record, 0.15 d) and up to 18 d (Ol-2), against about 7 h for a 0.5 mm cpx to reach a flat profile at 1200 °C and about 1.6 h for the final ascent recorded by H (Table 9.1).
  2. The minerals track the host. Olivine cores in the xenolith match the phenocrysts (6.89 and 5.95 ppm against 6.77 ppm). The opx interiors sit at 300–413 ppm, in equilibrium with a melt of 2.7–3.5 wt% H2O when the opx-anchored K is used, which is the range of the SR inclusions (Section 9.2). The cpx are wetter in wetter magma and hold 1.2–2.6× what the recalibrated experiments predict (Section 9.3).
  3. The signal that did enter is the host’s. Every grain lost water at its rim during final ascent (Chapter 8), and the interior grains see the drop with a lag that a fast transport network explains (Chapter 8).

Two limits apply. The cpx excess shows that partitioning is not settled, so the agreement is not quantitative. A cpx that retained mantle water would look the same as one that equilibrated with a wetter melt than the inclusions record, and no test here separates the two. We cannot exclude, but find no evidence for, a surviving mantle component in the cpx cores. The conclusion that nothing survives therefore rests mainly on the timescale argument, with the partitioning as a consistency check that is weaker than first reported.

The conclusion holds for hot alkali basalt carried in a cinder cone: a magma near 1200 °C with weeks between pickup and eruption. It says nothing about cooler or faster carriers.

Stage 0 leaves nothing to read, so the useful question becomes what this means for other studies.

10.2 What does this mean for xenolith water studies?

Hot alkali basalts of cinder cones are the worst case for fidelity. Diffusion of H+ through crystals, grain boundaries and melt is fast near 1200 °C, and the ascent from the mantle takes days to weeks. The minerals are reset. A study of xenoliths from such a host needs a measured host-magma water content, a P–T path and a degassing path before its mineral water can be read as mantle water. Cooler and faster hosts such as kimberlites, nephelinites and melilitites may preserve mantle water, but that needs constraints on their ascent rates, temperatures and water contents.

Published xenolith-NAM rates are upper bounds and not like-for-like. Several studies convert H loss from xenolith olivine into an ascent velocity. They fit a fixed boundary: zero H outside the crystal from entrainment onward, or a constant rim. They divide the full source depth (35–70 km) by the H-loss time. Our model, like those of Newcombe et al. (2020) and Barth et al. (2019), uses a boundary that follows the degassing melt, so H loss is confined to the shallow part of the path. When most of the loss is shallow, a velocity computed over the whole depth overstates the true constant velocity. Peslier et al. (2015) show the sensitivity inside one study: its rates rise 1.8× when the assumed start of loss moves from 1 to 1.8 GPa. The published rates also scale linearly with the diffusivity assumed, and Peslier & Luhr (2006) use a value that is the mean of two experimental values a factor of 10 apart.

Table 10.1 lists the published rates beside the SR olivine rate on the P–T path. The ratios run from 0.25 to 72: SR is slower than three studies, equal to Meerfelder Maar and faster than four. Because the published values are upper bounds, a ratio below 1 would shrink and one above 1 would grow if the studies were rerun with a degassing boundary, so SR is, if anything, faster relative to them than the table shows.

These rates are upper bounds because of their boundary condition, not by each study’s own label: most report central estimates, and one (Kilgore et al. 2020) a lower bound. The table shows the eight olivine H-loss rows of the thirteen published xenolith constraints; the Fe–Mg, Ar and settling constraints are omitted.

A consequence for earlier work is specific. Demouchy et al. (2006) derived ascent from 60–70 km in hours from Pali-Aike olivine. If the Pali-Aike host carried water comparable to SR, the H-loss time would belong to the final 6 km and the implied velocity would fall by an order of magnitude. The water content of that host is unknown, so this remains a hypothesis to test, but it shows why the host water and degassing path are the first things to measure.

Reset xenolith water is a loss for mantle studies. It is a gain for a different question, treated next.

10.3 What are xenoliths good for instead?

They are timekeepers of the final ascent. The same grains that lost their mantle water recorded the speed of the magma that carried them. On the P–T path the joint fit of olivine Ol-2 and the WHT1 exterior opx and cpx gives 0.100 MPa/s (0.050–0.200 over the slope envelope of 46–120 °C/GPa), 0.63× the isothermal 1200 °C joint rate of 0.158. At 0.10 MPa/s the magma rises about 3.6 m/s, and the stretch where the melt loses half its H2O (below about 55 MPa) takes minutes. The CO2/Nb start, the second scenario, gives 0.112 MPa/s.

Two clocks together give the whole path. Fe–Mg profiles in olivine give weeks of residence in the carrier, and the H profiles give a final ascent of about an hour and a half from 585 MPa. The thesis chapter described the contrast as acceleration from about 0.002 MPa/s through most of the path to about 0.2 MPa/s in the upper crust. The updated rate is 0.100 MPa/s on the cooling path, so the contrast is about 50× rather than 100×. The deep rate is the thesis estimate (at least a week from 1.15 GPa) and was not remeasured; the Fe–Mg times are residence times in the carrier, not ascent times.

The xenolith also records how the signal moved. The exterior grain saw the melt boundary directly (g 0.98), and the interior grains saw about half the drop. The effective network diffusivity, 10^-6.25 to 10^-5.75 m²/s at the headline rate from the two standard fits, is 3–9× below the olivine grain-boundary value of Demouchy (2010) (5 × 10^-6 m²/s). Xenoliths can thus constrain transport inside a magma body as well as its speed.

The SR rate sits with Seguam 1977 (0.93×) and at about 0.2× Fuego 1974 and Etna 3930 BP. It is 3.1–10× faster than Cerro Negro. These comparisons use the olivine-only path rate of 0.093 MPa/s against each study’s rate at its own temperature, not refits of the published studies (Chapter 8).
